Model explanation

Three dealers compete for client orders and each chooses an ask quote, a bid quote and an external trading rate. They can reduce inventory by attracting offsetting client orders (internalisation), or by trading in the external market (externalisation).

Prices and quotes

The unaffected price is driven by Brownian motion Wt with volatility σ. External trading adds a price displacement It, giving the market midprice Pt.

Pt∗=100+σWt
Pt=Pt∗+It

Dealer i quotes above or below the midprice using ask and bid depths δta,i and δtb,i. A smaller depth gives the client a more attractive price.

Sta,i=Pt+δta,i
Stb,i=Pt−δtb,i

Client flow and inventory

Common client buys and sells arrive as independent Poisson processes Zta and Ztb, with rates ρa and ρb. On either side k, the fraction allocated to dealer i depends on its depth relative to the average δ¯tk.

wtk,i=13[1−κ(δtk,i−δ¯tk)]

Here k is a for client buys and b for client sells. The parameter κ controls how strongly clients respond to quote differences. Each common order is divided across the three dealers, and each dealer also receives independent private orders Jtk,i at rate μ per side, giving its total client flow.

dZtk,i=wtk,idZtk+dJtk,i

Inventory Xti rises when clients sell to the dealer and falls when clients buy from it. External buying adds inventory, while external selling removes it.

dXti=dZtb,i−dZta,i+qtidt

External trading and price impact

The external trading rate qti is positive for buying and negative for selling. The dealers’ combined trading moves the midprice and impact then decays at rate β, while λ sets its sensitivity to trading.

dIt=(−βIt+λ∑j=13qtj)dt

The order imbalance plot shows Ztb−Zta, the cumulative common client sells minus buys.

What each dealer optimises

Each dealer balances expected trading profit against the cost of carrying inventory. For a finite planning period ending at T, it maximises

E[ΠTi−φi∫0T(Xti)2dt−αi(XTi)2]

Here ΠTi is trading profit after external execution costs, with terminal inventory valued at the unaffected price. The external execution cost includes ε2(qti)2 per unit of time. The penalties φi and αi discourage inventory during the planning period and at its end. In equilibrium, each dealer’s strategy is a best response to the others’ strategies.

The P&L curves show cumulative trading profit since the last reset, including common and private client orders and external execution costs. Remaining inventory is valued at the unaffected price.

Open loop Nash equilibrium

In the paper, we derive an open loop Nash equilibrium for the finite horizon model under the stated assumptions. This is a collection of strategies, one for each dealer, such that no dealer can improve its expected profit after inventory penalties by changing its own strategy alone.

Write ui for dealer i’s ask quotes, bid quotes and external trading rates, and u−i for the other dealers’ strategies. If Ji denotes the expected objective defined above, the equilibrium condition is

Ji(ui,u−i)≥Ji(vi,u−i)

This must hold for each of the three dealers and every admissible alternative strategy vi. The strategies may depend on information as it arrives. “Open loop” means that, when testing a dealer’s deviation, the other dealers’ control processes are held fixed and their actions are not recomputed as responses to the state changes caused by that deviation.

For a more detailed description of the model, the derivation of the Nash equilibrium, and our analysis, read the paper ↗.

Model explanation

A reference market maker optimises its quotes while competing with another dealer whose quotes follow a fixed rule. Each incoming client order is filled by either the reference market maker or the pooled competition.

The competitor’s quotes

The competitor shifts its quotes with its inventory Qtc and a Brownian noise term Zt. With base depths ι on both sides, its ask and bid depths relative to the unaffected price are

δ~ta=ι−βQtc−Zt
δ~tb=ι+βQtc+Zt

A positive inventory makes its ask cheaper and its bid less attractive, encouraging sales. Larger β strengthens this adjustment.

Winning client orders

Market buys and sells arrive at rates λa and λb. On either side k, the probability that the reference dealer fills an arriving order depends on its quoted depth relative to the competitor’s.

ptk=min{1,e−κ(δtk−δ~tk)}

A more competitive quote increases this probability, and if an order is not filled by the reference market maker then the competition fills the order.

Inventory and profit

The reference dealer’s P&L is its cash Ct plus inventory valued at the competitor’s midpoint Mtc.

Πt=Ct+QtMtc

This P&L excludes inventory penalties. The dealer’s optimisation also accounts for a running penalty controlled by φ and a terminal penalty controlled by γ. Cash and both inventories start at zero, and the initial unaffected price is 100.

For a more detailed description of the model, its solution and our analysis, read the paper ↗.

Model explanation

A market maker quotes to OTC clients while also having access to an internal exchange client’s sell order, and the dealer chooses both its streamed prices and when to buy from this internal order.

Streaming prices to OTC clients

The mid price follows Brownian motion. For each trade size z in 1, 5 and 10 million, the dealer sets bid and ask depths relative to the mid. Depths can be negative when quoting aggressively.

Stb,z=St−δtb,z
Sta,z=St+δta,z

OTC fills arrive at rates that decrease exponentially with quote depth, so more competitive prices attract more business.

Λi,z(δ)=λi,ze−κzδ

The baseline rates on each side are 0.2, 0.005 and 0.001 per second, with quote sensitivities 1.5, 1 and 0.5 per basis point, and the arrival rate control scales all these baseline rates together.

The internal order

The internal exchange client wants to sell at the mid plus an offset ρ. Each internal fill increases the dealer’s inventory by one million. After a fill, the visible unit is immediately replaced with probability p, and otherwise the displayed liquidity is depleted. While an order is present, the client can cancel it at rate ν.

Inventory and P&L

Let Ft be cumulative OTC client buys minus sells, weighted by size, and let Mt count internal purchases. Starting from zero inventory,

Qt=Mt−Ft
Πt=Xt+QtSt

P&L is cash plus inventory valued at the mid, shown in thousands of dollars. The optimisation additionally penalises inventory through φ and α, and outstanding internal liquidity through ψ. These penalties guide the strategy and are excluded from plotted P&L.

Choosing quotes and internal fills

We solve a stochastic control problem that compares continuing to quote with immediately taking internal liquidity. Its Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman quasi-variational inequality gives both the optimal quote depths and an inventory region where the dealer fills the internal order. The unfilled liquidity penalty encourages the dealer to facilitate the client’s execution.

Writing the value function as v=x+qs+h, the reduced HJBQVI is

with terminal condition h(T,q,l)=−αq2.

Model explanation

A trader starts with 20 units of 1,000 shares and posts sell quotes over a 300 second horizon. The initial midprice is $100 per share. Fill intensity decays with sensitivity k and passive impact with sensitivity m. The default m = k recovers the baseline model, and m can be adjusted independently.

Quotes, fills and passive impact

The trader quotes at the impacted midprice plus a depth δ. A smaller depth attracts more fills, but also creates a stronger downward drift in the midprice while inventory remains. Each fill sells one unit.

Λt=λe−kδt1{Qt−>0}
dSt=σdWt−ηe−mδt1{Qt−>0}dt

The blue price curve uses the same Brownian motion without the impact drift. The orange curve is the impacted mid, the purple curve is the sell quote, and green marks indicate fills. Quoting and additional impact stop once inventory reaches zero.

The execution objective

The trader maximises expected proceeds plus the value of remaining inventory, with running and terminal inventory penalties. In the paper’s unit normalisation, X sums the per share prices received for filled units.

𝔼[XT+QTST−αQT2−φ∫0TQt2dt]

Any inventory left at the horizon is valued at the impacted midprice.

The optimal quote

Write the value function as u=x+qs+θ and let Δq(t)=θ(t,q)−θ(t,q−1). For m ≠ k, the paper’s solution uses the principal real branch of the Lambert W function, defined by W0(z)eW0(z)=z.

Φq(z)=W0(m−kkηλqmexp(−m−kk(1+kz)))
δ*(t,q)=1k+Δq(t)+Φq(Δq(t))m−k

The correction θ solves a nonlinear system backward from the terminal inventory penalty.

∂tθq=φq2−λ(1k+Φq(Δq(t))m)e−kδ*(t,q)
θ(t,0)=0,θ(T,q)=−αq2

When m = k, this reduces to the original quote below, with θ = log(ω) / k obtained from the paper’s linear system.

δ*(t,q)=1k+Δq(t)+ηλq

Implementation shortfall

As defined in the paper, implementation shortfall compares the initial price with the average price of completed fills. With N filled units,

ISt=S0−XtNt,Nt>0

It is shown in dollars per share. Positive values mean execution below the initial price, and negative values mean execution above it. The quantity is undefined until the first fill and excludes inventory penalties and any unfilled shares.

Model explanation

A dealer receives a continuous flow of inventory and trades to unwind it over one trading day. The incoming flow has a changing drift, called toxicity, which reacts to the dealer’s own trades. This demonstration includes the notebook’s observed price signal.

Inflow, hedging and inventory

The trading rate q is positive for buying and negative for selling. Cumulative hedging Q adds to incoming flow Z to give the dealer’s inventory X.

dQt=qtdt,Xt=x+Qt+Zt

Inflow fluctuates around its drift θ. A negative coefficient a makes toxicity revert towards zero, while positive a produces momentum. The coefficient b describes how the dealer’s hedging changes future inflow, c couples toxicity to the observed price signal, and d controls random changes in toxicity.

dZt=θtdt+σdWtZ
dθt=(aθt+bqt+cUt)dt+ddWtθ

Prices and impact

The unaffected price P combines Brownian fluctuations with the observed signal A, which has drift U following a mean reverting Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. Setting ℓ = 0 stops new signal noise, but the current signal continues to decay. Since the signal starts at zero and reverts to it, resetting with ℓ = 0 removes the signal entirely.

dAt=Utdt,dUt=−κUtdt+ℓdWtU

Trading creates transient impact Y, which accumulates at strength λ and decays at rate β. The Brownian motions driving price, signal, flow and toxicity are independent. Setting c = 0 removes the signal’s direct effect on toxicity while retaining its effect on price.

dPt=Utdt+σMdWtM,dYt=(−βYt+λqt)dt

The price plot compares P with P + Y, as in the notebook. The execution price also includes instantaneous impact, controlled by ε.

St=Pt+Yt+ε2qt

The trading objective

The dealer minimises expected trading costs, less the value of terminal inventory, plus a penalty for any inventory remaining at the end of the day. Inventory is penalised rather than constrained to finish at zero.

infq𝔼[∫0TStqtdt−PTXT+αXT2]

Partial and full information

Under partial information, the dealer observes inflow, inventory, prices and the signal U but cannot directly observe θ. Its conditional estimate θ̂ is updated using the difference between observed inflow and the flow predicted by its current estimate.

dθ^t=(aθ^t+bqt+cUt)dt+vtσ2(dZt−θ^tdt)

Here, v is the toxicity estimation error variance.

dvtdt=2avt−vt2σ2+d2,v0=0

With full information, the dealer observes θ directly, so θ̂ = θ and the two toxicity curves coincide.

Optimal unwinding

The optimal strategy is linear in observed inventory, estimated toxicity, transient impact, the unaffected price and the signal U.

qt*=gX(t)Xt+gθ(t)θ^t+gY(t)Yt+gP(t)Pt+gU(t)Ut

The deterministic coefficients solve the trading problem backwards from the terminal cost. Under full information, the strategy uses θ in place of θ̂.
