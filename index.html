Model explanation

Three dealers compete for client orders. Each chooses an ask quote, a bid quote and an external trading rate. They can reduce inventory by attracting offsetting client orders (internalisation), or by trading in the external market (externalisation). This simulation uses the full information, impact aware model from my working paper.

Prices and quotes

The unaffected price is driven by Brownian motion Wt with volatility σ. External trading adds a price displacement It, giving the market midprice Pt.

Pt∗=100+σWt
Pt=Pt∗+It

Dealer i quotes above or below the midprice using ask and bid depths δta,i and δtb,i. A smaller depth gives the client a more attractive price.

Sta,i=Pt+δta,i
Stb,i=Pt−δtb,i

Client flow and inventory

Common client buys and sells arrive as independent Poisson processes Zta and Ztb, with rates ρa and ρb. On either side k, the fraction allocated to dealer i depends on its depth relative to the average δ¯tk.

wtk,i=13[1−κ(δtk,i−δ¯tk)]

Here k is a for client buys and b for client sells. The parameter κ controls how strongly clients respond to quote differences. Each common order is divided across the three dealers. Each dealer also receives independent private orders Jtk,i at rate μ per side, giving its total client flow.

dZtk,i=wtk,idZtk+dJtk,i

Inventory Xti rises when clients sell to the dealer and falls when clients buy from it. External buying adds inventory, while external selling removes it.

dXti=dZtb,i−dZta,i+qtidt

The linear allocation rule is used without clipping. Large quote differences can produce shares outside zero to one, which is a limitation of this approximation.

External trading and price impact

The external trading rate qti is positive for buying and negative for selling. The dealers’ combined trading moves the midprice. Impact then decays at rate β, while λ sets its sensitivity to trading.

dIt=(−βIt+λ∑j=13qtj)dt

The order imbalance plot shows Ztb−Zta, the cumulative common client sells minus buys. Private orders are excluded from that plot.

What each dealer optimises

Each dealer balances expected trading profit against the cost of carrying inventory. For a finite planning period ending at T, it maximises

E[ΠTi−φi∫0T(Xti)2dt−αi(XTi)2]

Here ΠTi is trading profit after external execution costs, with terminal inventory valued at the unaffected price. The external execution cost includes ε2(qti)2 per unit of time. The penalties φi and αi discourage inventory during the planning period and at its end. In equilibrium, each dealer’s strategy is a best response to the others’ strategies.

How the simulation chooses its actions

All three dealers observe every inventory and the current impact. These quantities form the state

zt=(Xt1,Xt2,Xt3,It,1)⊤

The working model’s coupled nonlinear coefficient equations determine matrices that turn this state into the three dealers’ quote depths and external trading rates.

δta=Da(H)zt− δtb=Db(H)zt− qt=Q(H)zt

The matrices are evaluated at the chosen planning horizon H. For continuous playback, this horizon stays fixed as the state changes. This uses the finite horizon solution as a rolling policy rather than claiming an equilibrium for an infinite trading period. The notation t− means the quotes use the state just before an arrival.

Using the controls

Each dealer slider changes its running and terminal inventory penalties together, so φi=αi. Larger values make carrying inventory more costly. The market controls set arrival rates, volatility, quote sensitivity, impact, external execution cost and the planning horizon.

Inventories and impact start at zero. The charts show the latest two units of model time, with solid ask quotes and dashed bid quotes. Parameter changes preserve the current state. Reset starts a fresh path with the selected parameters. Speed changes only playback, and the animation pauses when out of view.
